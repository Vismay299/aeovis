01Executive summary

The one-page version to read before anything else.

The shift is real and it is monetizable. Buyers increasingly get their answers from ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews, Gemini and Perplexity instead of a page of blue links. When the AI answers without naming your client, the click — and the sale — never happens. A whole category ("Answer Engine Optimization" / "Generative Engine Optimization") has formed around this in under two years, led by Profound, which just raised a $96M Series C at a $1B valuation. The category is now large enough that Gartner published its first Market Guide for it, and consolidation has started (Sitecore bought Scrunch AI for ~$225M; Adobe is buying Semrush for ~$1.9B).

Our two-part product is the right shape. Part 1 — the AI Visibility Score — is the cheap, alarming, foot-in-the-door diagnostic ("AI recommends your competitor, not you"). Part 2 — the GEO Operating System — is a human-in-the-loop team of agents that audits the client's site, writes content engineered to win AI citations, publishes it to their WordPress after a human approves, and re-checks every couple of weeks to keep them on top. Part 1 sells the problem; Part 2 is the recurring revenue and the retention.

The core strategic read

The funded leaders (Profound, Bluefish) are enterprise-first and measurement-heavy. Almost nobody credibly does affordable, done-for-you, generate-and-publish for SMBs — especially on WordPress. That is our lane. We do not out-Profound Profound; we serve the Indian and long-tail SMB/D2C/SaaS market they will never chase, at a price (~₹40,000/mo) that only works because our agents replace the human hours a traditional agency would burn.

03What we have today — the current build

An honest, complete inventory of Part 1 (the Visibility Score). This is what actually runs.

Perceev today is a Next.js 14 (App Router, plain JavaScript) app on Supabase that measures one thing well: how visible a brand is inside Google AI Overviews, in the India geo. It is mid-rebrand (aeovis → Perceev, dark "dossier" redesign) and was recently rebuilt from a sprawling multi-engine "v7" into a focused "v8" pipeline.

  • Prompt generation. For each brand we generate exactly 30 unbranded prompts — the messy, real things a buyer types, not the brand name. The site is scraped (Firecrawl) for context, then the free OpenRouter model (google/gemma-4-31b-it:free) writes queries across intent tiers (informational / commercial / comparison / problem-aware), topped up if short from People-Also-Ask, Google autocomplete, and a static 30-industry pack.
  • Probing. Each prompt is run through Bright Data's SERP API with brd_ai_overview=2 (India, gl=in), at concurrency 6. For every prompt we record: did an AI Overview appear, its full text, its cited sources, whether the brand is named in the text, and whether the brand's domain is cited.
  • Scoring & rivals. Straight ratios (below) plus one batched OpenRouter call to extract competitors from the collected AI-Overview text, and an aggregation of the most-cited domains.
  • Persistence. Results are written to Supabase; a SHA-256 hash of the prompt set gives each scan a stable identity so scores are comparable over time.

3.2 · The scoring model Built

Deliberately not a mysterious 0–100 composite. It is three clean numbers, each on its own fixed denominator — because mixing denominators was the recurring historical bug. "Visible" = the brand is named in the AI Overview text or its domain is cited.

MetricDefinitionReads as

There are reliability gates: if the error rate is over 50%, or fewer than 15% of prompts returned an AI Overview, the dashboard suppresses the score and shows "not enough AI Overview data" rather than a misleading number. Tiers: strong ≥ 50, moderate ≥ 25, weak < 25.

3.3 · The dashboard Built

The rebranded dashboard has a sidebar and five views: Overview (hero score, KPIs, "where you're losing"), Answers (the verbatim AI-Overview text with the brand highlighted — this is the "proof" screen a salesperson shows), Rivals (competitor co-mention leaderboard), Sources (which domains AI cites most), and Queries (manage the tracked prompt set).

3. The async job model + daily cron is exactly the backbone a periodic agent loop needs.

04Honest assessment — what's weak and what to fix

The things I'd want a co-founder to know before we sell this, roughly in priority order.

Fix first  Only one of four promised engines is live

The landing copy and the pitch imply ChatGPT / Gemini / Perplexity / Google AI Mode. Only Google AI Overviews works. This is the single biggest gap between the story and the product — and, happily, the cheapest to close (§3 hidden assets, §11 Phase 1).

Credibility risk  Reliability gates were loosened

To cope with India's volatile AI-Overview coverage, the "is this scan trustworthy?" threshold was relaxed (minimum coverage 30% → 15%). It made healthy scans stop getting flagged — but it also means a genuinely thin scan can now show a confident-looking score. For a paid product where the number is the whole pitch, we need to re-examine this and be transparent about confidence.

Blocks selling  No accounts, no multi-tenancy, no billing

Everything is keyed by brand name in sessionStorage with wide-open anonymous database access. That is fine for a demo and impossible for a paid SaaS with client data. Auth + per-account isolation + billing is prerequisite work for charging anyone.

Quality ceiling  The free LLM is fine for prompts, not for client content

The free OpenRouter model is a smart cost choice for generating test prompts. But the moment Part 2 writes content a client will publish under their own name, a free model is the wrong tool — quality and factual reliability matter too much. We must budget for a capable model on the paid path (§10).

Tech debt  Orphaned code & one inconsistency

  • The rebrand left orphaned components (the planned "dossier" landing was built but never wired; several old dashboard panels linger).
  • /api/opportunities still calls the legacy Gemini path while everything else standardised on OpenRouter — a small but real inconsistency to clean up when we revive that feature.

Methodology  AIO text ≠ what a real ChatGPT user sees

We infer "visibility" from the AI-Overview block returned to a scraper. That is directionally correct and cheap, but it is not a personalised, logged-in consumer session. Profound's differentiator is literally "what you see is what your customer sees." We should be honest about our methodology and treat consumer-fidelity capture as a premium upgrade, not pretend we already have it (§10).

Net

None of this is fatal — it's a well-built diagnostic that needs productizing (auth, billing, confidence, more engines) before it's a business, plus a Part 2 to justify the price. The bones are good and unusually far along for a pre-revenue product.

Profound coined "Answer Engine Optimization" and organises its suite as Monitor → Create → Operate:

  • Prompt Volumes — their real moat: a licensed dataset of 1.5B+ anonymised AI conversations (from opt-in consumer panels), so they can tell you which prompts actually have volume and intent. This is data we cannot cheaply replicate.
  • Agent Analytics — server-log analytics of AI crawler bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot) hitting your site, with spoof detection.
  • Shopping — visibility inside ChatGPT Shopping (SKU-level).
  • Agents + Workflows + Aim — the "write" side: agents research across 16 models, draft optimised content, and publish to WordPress/Sanity/Contentful with a human approval step; "Aim" is an always-on agent that turns findings into execution plans.

How their tech works: for insights they capture responses directly from the browser ("what you see is what your customer sees") rather than raw APIs; for prompt volumes they license conversation data. Under the hood: Aho-Corasick pattern matching, a RAG pipeline over a Milvus vector DB, and self-hosted GPU inference. Pricing: Starter $99/mo (ChatGPT only, 50 prompts), Growth $399/mo (3 engines, 100 prompts), Enterprise custom (~$2,000–$5,000+/mo). Their metrics mirror ours almost exactly: Visibility = responses with your brand ÷ responses with any brand; Share of Voice = your mentions ÷ all brand mentions.

What to copy vs. what to skip

Copy: their metric definitions (validates ours), the daily-run cadence framing, and the Monitor→Create→Operate story arc. Skip: the licensed 1.5B-conversation dataset, ~10-engine breadth, bot-log analytics, and shopping — all expensive, enterprise-grade, and unnecessary for our buyer. We compete on delivery and price, not data volume.

06Product vision — two verticals, one funnel

How the score and the operating system feed each other.

1The Visibility Score

The hook. Cheap or free, fast, and alarming.

A prospect enters a URL and sees, in the AI's own words, that it recommends competitors instead of them — across ChatGPT, Google AIO, Gemini and Perplexity. This creates the "I'm losing money right now" feeling. It's a lead magnet and a diagnostic, not the revenue.

2The GEO Operating System

The value. Recurring, human-in-the-loop, sticky.

The subscription that actually fixes the score: audit → generate citation-worthy content → human approves → publish to their WordPress → re-measure → repeat. This is the ₹40k/mo, and the reason they don't churn.

The funnel is: free/cheap score → "here's exactly what's wrong and what a competitor is doing right" → done-for-you subscription to fix it → proof, every two weeks, that the score is climbing. The score is the marketing; the OS is the business. Crucially, the score's output (the 30 prompts, the gaps, the winning source pages) is the exact input the OS needs — so the two halves aren't separate products bolted together, they're one loop.

Positioning line for the deck

"Profound tells a Fortune 500 team what's wrong and hands it to their in-house marketers. Perceev is the whole team — we find what's wrong and our agents plus a human fix it and publish it, for the price of a freelancer."

07The GEO/SEO Operating System — how it works

The core of Part 2: a human-in-the-loop team of specialised agents, grounded in what actually wins AI citations.

7.1 · The philosophy — optimise passages, not pages

Before the architecture, the strategy the agents execute. AI answers are not "top-10 blue links summarised." The engine takes one prompt, fans it out into 8–15 synthetic sub-queries, retrieves content at the passage (paragraph) level, and stitches passages from ~5 sites into one answer. So we optimise quotable passages, not whole pages. The evidence on what moves citations is unusually concrete:

LeverEffect on AI visibilityWhat the agents do about it
Add quotations+41%Every draft includes quotable expert lines.
Add statistics+34%Cite hard numbers with sources near the top of each section.
Cite sources+30%Reference authoritative sources inline.
Brand mentions vs backlinks~3× strongerOff-page workstream targets unlinked brand mentions / digital PR, not just links.
Freshness~26% fresherContent-refresh is a first-class agent action, not an afterthought.
Keyword stuffingnegligibleExplicitly not done — the old SEO trick doesn't transfer.

Source-type bias is real too: government (11.75×), e-commerce (5.1×), documentation (3.4×), and news (2.6×) pages are over-cited relative to rank, and Reddit/forums are cited in ~40% of AI answers. Structured data (schema) is table-stakes hygiene, not a primary lever. The agents encode all of this as their playbook.

7.2 · The closed loop

1Visibility scan→ 2Master plans→ 3Audit + Create→ 4Reviewer QA→ 5Human approves→ 6Publish to WP (draft)→ 7Re-scan + measure lift↺

Steps 6–7 reuse what we already have: the content_events table records what was published, and contentLift.js measures whether the score for the related prompts went up. The loop then repeats on a cadence (§9), which is what keeps a client at the top instead of a one-and-done project.

7.5 · The failure mode we must engineer against

The #1 technical risk, named

In real multi-agent content systems, the best-documented failure is that the writer agents favour SEO-optimised "slop" over authoritative sources — which is exactly the low-quality content AI engines are learning to discount. If our agents produce generic AI filler, we actively hurt clients and lose the account. Mitigation is non-negotiable: bake explicit source-authority and originality heuristics into both the Creator's brief and the Reviewer's rubric, keep the human gate mandatory, and never auto-publish live. Other guardrails from the research: schema-validate every hand-off between agents, keep tasks few and sharply specified, and cap autonomous steps (most production systems run ≤10 steps before a human).

7.6 · Build choices

  • Frameworks: prototype the content "crew" fast in CrewAI; build the durable orchestrator + approval gate in LangGraph (checkpointing and native human-in-the-loop are precisely what a publish pipeline needs). Avoid classic AutoGen (now in maintenance).
  • Models: tiered on purpose — the free model rule stays for the scoring product's prompt-gen, but the paid OS needs a capable model for Master/Creator/Reviewer. This also fits our working style (strong model to plan, cheaper model to execute the grunt work).
  • Human-in-the-loop is a feature, not a fallback. "A human reviews and publishes it themselves" is our quality guarantee and our differentiator against pure-automation tools — we sell it, we don't hide it.

08WordPress plug-and-play

"They subscribe, we connect to their WordPress, understand what they've built, and start improving it."

8.1 · How "connect your WordPress" actually works

WordPress ships everything we need out of the box. The pragmatic MVP path:

  1. The client enters their site URL + username and generates an Application Password in WP admin (built into WordPress 5.6+; a per-integration token, individually revocable, no plugin required).
  2. We authenticate to the WordPress REST API over HTTPS and store the token encrypted at rest.
  3. "Understand what they've built" = we read their existing posts, pages and categories via the REST API + sitemap. That's the content inventory the Technical Audit and Master agents work from — the "plug-and-play understanding" the client is promised.
  4. All generated content is pushed as a draft (status=draft) — never published live automatically. The client's human (or ours) reviews and hits publish.

8.2 · Two phases of integration

AMVP — Application Password

Ship this first. Zero plugin, works on any self-hosted WP.

REST API + token, draft-first. A short setup guide gets a client connected in minutes. Good enough to onboard the first paying clients.

BPremium — Perceev plugin

The "one-click connect" upgrade, later.

A branded plugin gives a single "Connect" button, tightly scoped permissions, and webhooks so their site tells us when content changes (triggering a re-audit). Nicer UX, more support burden — do it once volume justifies it.

Security — do this correctly from day one

We are holding write-access to clients' live websites. Rules: HTTPS only; encrypt tokens, never log them; least privilege — tell clients to create a dedicated Editor user, not Admin; one revocable token per site so offboarding is clean; and draft-first everything as defence-in-depth. Skip self-hosted OAuth (heavy, brittle) at MVP. Handle sites whose security plugins disable the REST API with a clear error path.

09The periodic loop — cadence & automation

The flywheel that keeps a client ranked, and why we run two clocks not one.

The key design move is to decouple the two loops, because they have very different costs:

LoopCadenceCostWhy
Scan & scoreEvery 10–15 days (biweekly)Cents — negligibleFrequency is the product: trend lines, "your score moved," alerting. Cheap enough to do often.
Re-audit + regenerate contentMonthly, or event-triggered on a score dropLLM tokens + human review timeThis is the real cost. Do it deliberately, not constantly.

So: scan biweekly, regenerate monthly. A 10–15 day scoring cadence is a reasonable, marketable default ("we check your AI visibility every two weeks"). We already have the primitive — the daily Vercel cron and async job model — so extending it to "for every active subscription, re-scan on schedule and, if the score slipped, wake the Master agent" is an incremental build, not a new system.

Why this is the retention engine

AI answers are volatile — rivals publish, engines change what they cite, Reddit threads rise and fall. A one-time content project decays. The periodic loop reframes the product as "we keep you at the top," which is a subscription a client renews, not a project they finish. That is the whole reason ₹40k/mo recurring is defensible.

10.2 · Is ₹40,000/mo (~$480) the right price?

Yes, and it's well-placed:

  • It sits at the entry of the Indian "local SEO" band (₹40k–1.2L/mo) and just above a standard ₹30k content package — credible, not cheap-looking, to an Indian SMB/D2C buyer.
  • Globally it's below the US small-business GEO floor (~$1,500/mo), so to anyone paying in USD it reads as aggressive value for a "GEO OS + done-for-you content."
  • For reference, US GEO/AEO retainers run $1,500–$50,000+/mo; Indian content runs ₹2,500–8,000 per 1,000-word article.
The catch — and the whole thesis

At ₹40k/mo the margin only works if the automation genuinely replaces human hours. A traditional agency delivering ₹40k of manual GEO + content is near break-even. Our multi-agent system — auto-audit, auto-draft, human only reviews — is precisely what turns that into a profitable price. The agents aren't a gimmick; they are the margin. If we can't keep human time per client low, the price doesn't work — so keeping the Reviewer agent good enough that a human can approve in minutes is a business-critical metric, not a nice-to-have.

  • Run it hands-on for one friendly client; measure content lift with the existing content_events/contentLift tools.
Phase 3 · Orchestrate & automate the loopmargin

Make it run itself between human approvals.

  • Introduce the Master + Orchestrator (LangGraph) with schema-validated hand-offs and the mandatory human gate.
  • Wire the periodic loop (biweekly scan, monthly regenerate, score-drop triggers) onto the cron.
  • Drive down human-minutes-per-client — the metric the ₹40k price depends on.
Phase 4 · Productize & scalegrowth

From done-for-you service to plug-and-play product.

  • Ship the branded WordPress plugin (one-click connect + webhooks).
  • Onboarding self-serve, agency/reseller tier, the free-score funnel at scale.
  • Consider a premium consumer-fidelity capture tier if enterprise-ish clients demand it.
Sequencing logic

Phases 0–1 make the existing asset earn money and close the credibility gap cheaply. Phase 2 proves Part 2 with one client and real numbers before we over-invest in orchestration. Only then (Phase 3) do we automate, and only after that (Phase 4) do we productize. Each phase de-risks the next and is independently valuable.

13Recommendations — what to decide now

The short list for the co-founder conversation.

  1. Commit to the two-part product and the SMB/India/WordPress lane. Don't chase Profound's enterprise, data-heavy game — we can't win it and don't need to.
  2. Do Phase 0 before anything shiny. Auth, billing, honest confidence. An un-sellable diagnostic is not a business, however good the demo.
  3. Light up the other three engines next — it's cheap, it's mostly wiring, and it closes the biggest gap between our pitch and our product.
  4. Prove Part 2 with one design-partner client (semi-manual) before building the full orchestration. Real content-lift numbers are worth more than a clever architecture diagram.
  5. Make "a human reviews and publishes" a selling point, not a limitation. It's our quality guarantee and our edge over pure-automation tools.
  6. Hold the line on ₹40k/mo — and treat "human-minutes per client" as the number that makes or breaks the unit economics.
Bottom line

We have an unusually mature Part 1 for a pre-revenue product, a competitor set that has proven the demand and left the SMB done-for-you lane open, and a codebase that already contains the seeds of Part 2. The work ahead is productizing and delivering, not inventing. That's a good place to be.
